OpenAI’s Persistent AI Agent: What Always-On AI Could Mean for Users

OpenAI is reportedly testing a persistent AI agent that can keep working on tasks. Here’s what always-on AI could mean for the future.

Staff Writer

AI assistants are getting better at answering questions, writing code and helping with everyday tasks. But the next big change may be less about what an AI can do in a single conversation and more about how long it can keep working.

OpenAI is reportedly testing a more persistent mode for its Codex AI agent. The idea is straightforward: instead of stopping after a limited period, an AI agent could continue working on an assigned task until the user tells it to stop.

That sounds like a small feature. It could actually change how people think about AI assistants.

What is a persistent AI agent?

A persistent AI agent is an AI system designed to continue working on a task over a longer period instead of waiting for a new prompt after every step.

Today, many AI tools follow a simple pattern. You ask something, the AI responds, and the interaction ends.

A persistent agent works differently.

You could give it a goal and allow it to continue making progress while following the permissions and limits set by the user.

According to reporting about OpenAI's testing, the experimental persistent mode for Codex is designed around this idea.

Why does this matter?

The difference becomes obvious when a task takes hours rather than minutes.

Imagine asking an AI coding agent to work through a large software project. Instead of stopping after completing one section, a persistent system could continue checking files, running tests, fixing problems and moving to the next approved task.

The same concept could eventually be useful for research, writing, data analysis and other digital workflows.

In other words, AI could become less like a chatbot and more like a digital worker that stays focused on a particular assignment.

How would an always-on AI work?

An always-on AI system would need more than a powerful language model.

It would need memory, task planning, permissions and a way to keep track of what it has already done.

For example, an agent working on a project might need to remember:

  • What the original goal was
  • Which tasks have already been completed
  • What problems were discovered
  • What should happen next
  • Which actions require user approval

This makes persistent AI much closer to an automated workflow system than a traditional chatbot.

Could AI start tasks by itself?

This is one of the more interesting possibilities.

Reports about the experimental system suggest that OpenAI is also exploring greater proactivity, where an agent could identify useful follow-up work based on previous interactions.

That does not necessarily mean the AI would have unlimited freedom.

A responsible system would still need clear permissions and boundaries.

For example, an agent might be allowed to organize files or prepare a report automatically while requiring confirmation before taking an important external action.

Persistent AI could change coding

Software development is one of the clearest areas where persistent agents could become useful.

A developer might assign an AI agent a project and allow it to work through a list of approved tasks.

The agent could inspect the existing code, make changes, run tests and investigate failures. If something goes wrong, it could potentially keep working on the problem instead of simply returning an error message to the developer.

This could make AI coding assistants considerably more useful for larger projects.

What about everyday users?

The technology could eventually move beyond programming.

A persistent AI assistant could potentially help manage long-running digital projects such as organizing information, preparing research, monitoring approved workflows or maintaining a personal task list.

The important word is potentially.

Persistent AI is still an evolving area, and giving an AI more freedom also creates more opportunities for mistakes.

The biggest problem: trust

Making an AI work for a long time is not necessarily difficult compared with making sure it works correctly.

A small mistake made once is one thing. A mistake repeated by an autonomous system for several hours could become much more serious.

That means persistent AI needs strong safeguards.

Users should be able to understand what the agent is doing, stop it easily and control which actions it is allowed to perform.

Good AI agents will therefore need to balance autonomy with user control.

Could persistent AI replace traditional apps?

Probably not completely, but it could change how we use them.

Instead of opening several applications and manually moving information between them, users could increasingly tell an AI agent what they want accomplished.

The agent could then use approved tools to complete the individual steps.

That would represent a major shift in computing.

Today, people usually learn how software works. In an agent-first future, people may increasingly describe the result they want and let AI handle the process.

Why AI companies are moving toward agents

There is a clear industry-wide movement toward agentic AI.

Google has been developing AI systems designed to work with tools and robotics, while Anthropic recently introduced a hardware standard intended to connect AI agents with physical devices. 1

OpenAI's reported work on persistent agents fits into the same broader trend.

The goal is no longer simply to build an AI that gives impressive answers. Companies increasingly want systems that can take useful actions.

Will persistent AI be available to everyone?

That is still unclear for experimental features.

Testing a persistent agent is very different from releasing one to millions of users. Before a system like this becomes widely available, developers need to solve difficult questions around reliability, security, privacy, permissions and cost.

An agent that runs continuously could also require considerably more computing resources than a normal question-and-answer interaction.

What could the future look like?

The long-term vision is easy to imagine.

You could give an AI assistant a broad objective in the morning, check its progress during the day and review the finished work later.

Instead of asking AI dozens of small questions, you would give it a goal and let it manage the individual steps.

That is the promise of persistent AI.

But the technology will only become genuinely useful if people can trust it.

Final thoughts

OpenAI's reported persistent AI work highlights an important change in the AI industry.

The next generation of assistants may not simply become better at answering questions. They may become better at staying focused, remembering context and completing longer tasks.

That could make AI dramatically more useful for coding, research and everyday digital work.

At the same time, persistent AI raises an important question: how much independence should we actually give an AI system?

The answer will probably depend on the task.

For low-risk work, people may eventually want AI to operate with very little supervision. For sensitive or important actions, users will likely expect much tighter controls.

Either way, the move toward persistent AI suggests that the future of assistants may be less about asking AI a question and more about giving AI a job.
